    How Peanut's Needle works

    This is a just-for-fun forecast for people who like election-night needles. The Live count tab shows only the county's actual numbers; this tab estimates where those numbers will end up. It's an homage to The New York Times' election needle and isn't affiliated with or endorsed by the Times. Peanut is a small doodle and the needle's mascot. Peanut's mood shows how sure the forecast is, not who's ahead, and Peanut does not do the math: the model described below does.

    What goes in

    Only what San Mateo County publishes for Measure Y at each update: the YES and NO totals and the county's "Website Updated" time. Nothing about individual voters is used or stored.

    The idea: three groups of ballots

    • 8:10 PM: early mail. The first report is every mail ballot the county received through Sunday, Nov 1. Its YES share is Peanut's baseline.
    • Election night (9 PM, then hourly): in-person ballots. These updates add only ballots cast at vote centers. In past county elections those voters were less supportive of local measures than mail voters, so Peanut expects YES to slip during the night.
    • Nov 5 to Nov 16: late mail. Mail dropped off on Election Day or arriving by Nov 10, plus provisional ballots and signature fixes. Late mail tends to come from younger voters, who are less likely to own homes, and on property taxes it has run more YES than early mail, so Peanut expects YES to recover after election night.

    Each group gets a "lean": how many points more or less YES it is than the early mail. Peanut starts each lean from county history and updates it from every new county update (a standard Bayesian update; small updates count for less, and the lean is allowed to drift a little from one update to the next).

    What county history says

    • In-person ballots run less YES. Across 70 San Mateo County measures since 2020 with at least 200 in-person votes, vote-center ballots ran about 4.5 points less YES than mail ballots on average. On Belmont's own city measures the gap was 15.5 points in 2022 and 6.9 points in 2024. (Computed from the county's certified results.)
    • Late ballots have moved property taxes toward YES. In 12 county property-tax measures (parcel taxes, school bonds and one city bond) where an election-night count could be paired with the final count, YES gained every time, by 0.8 to 7.3 points. In the March 2020 primary, YES finished 2 to 10 points higher than the county's own 8:56 PM election-night snapshot on all seven school measures. (Pairs compiled from local news reports and the county's results.)
    • Measure Y is unusual. It's a citizen-initiated parcel tax that passes with a simple majority, paid by the square foot, with an exemption for senior homeowners who apply. That exemption may narrow the usual gap between early and late mail, so Peanut assumes a smaller late shift than history shows.

    How many votes are left

    Belmont has about 17,400 registered voters. Peanut combines a turnout estimate (about 11,300 votes on Measure Y, give or take 11%) with how much of the final count the county usually has done by each release. The 8:10 PM report and each post-election update pin this down a little more (the in-person updates on election night don't change it); by the last scheduled update only a few percent should remain.

    Then it simulates

    Peanut plays out 20,000 versions of the rest of the count from those uncertainties, with fat tails so big surprises stay possible, and counts how often YES ends above 50%. That's the chance on the needle. Every forecast uses the same random numbers, so everyone sees the same chance for the same count, and the needle moves only when the count does.

    The wobble

    On the Live count tab the wobble is just for fun. Here it means something. Peanut reruns the forecast 96 times with its assumptions (how the uncounted ballots lean, how fast the county counts, how many people vote) spread across their own uncertainty, and the needle wanders across the middle half of the chances that come out. A wide wobble means the chance itself depends a lot on assumptions; when the outcome no longer depends on them, the needle sits still. The button under the needle turns it off.

    Does it work?

    We replayed 23 real San Mateo County ballot questions from 2020 to 2026 (21 local measures, a city council recall and state Proposition 13), giving Peanut only partial counts from election night (one from two days later), with its turnout guess deliberately 10% off. For eight Nov 2022 measures, the 8:10 PM mail-only count had to be rebuilt from news reports and the final in-person totals. Peanut's 80% ranges contained the certified result in 26 of 32 forecasts, and every forecast made after the first update. On property taxes and school bonds the final results still came in about 2.5 points more YES than Peanut's middle estimate, mostly in two primaries with unusually large late surges. These same elections helped set Peanut's assumptions, so expect it to do somewhat worse on a new one. On made-up election nights drawn from Peanut's own assumptions, its 80% ranges caught the result about 9 times in 10. That's cautious on purpose: Peanut also budgets for surprises those made-up nights don't contain. When the made-up nights were 60% more chaotic than Peanut assumes, its 80% ranges still caught the result about 3 times in 4.

    What could go wrong

    • The late ballots might not lean YES this time. If they break even, Peanut will be too optimistic about YES until the first post-election update on Nov 5 shows it.
    • The county could count in a different order than usual, or post a correction.
    • If the 8:10 PM report is missed or stamped late, the baseline includes some in-person ballots and is a bit muddier.

    Want to argue with Peanut?

    Every assumption lives in one file, data/peanut-priors.json, in the project's GitHub repository. Suggest a change there with your reasoning (aggregate numbers and public sources only). The model is in js/peanut-model.js and the tests are in tests/.

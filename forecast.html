    How Peanut's Needle works

    This is a just-for-fun forecast for people who like election-night needles. The Live count tab shows only the county's actual numbers; this tab guesses where those numbers will end up. It's an homage to The New York Times' election needle and isn't affiliated with or endorsed by the Times. Peanut is a small doodle and the needle's mascot. Peanut does not do math; the code below does.

    What goes in

    Only what San Mateo County publishes for Measure Y at each update: the YES and NO totals and the county's "Website Updated" time. Nothing about individual voters is used or stored.

    The idea

    • The first report is the baseline. The 8:10 PM report is mail ballots the county had by Sunday. Those voters skew older and more settled, so their YES share isn't the final answer, but it's the anchor.
    • Later ballots come in two batches that can lean differently. Election-night updates (mostly people who voted in person at vote centers, plus more mail) and post-election updates (mail dropped off on Election Day or arriving late, and provisional ballots). Each batch gets a "lean": how much more or less YES it is than the early mail.
    • The leans start from San Mateo County history and learn from the night. Before a batch is counted, Peanut uses a prior from past county elections. As increments arrive, Peanut compares each one's YES share with the baseline and updates the lean (a standard Bayesian normal update, noisier for small increments).
    • How many votes are left combines a turnout estimate for Belmont with how much of its final count the county usually finishes by each release. Early on this is uncertain; by the last scheduled update it's small.
    • Then it simulates. Peanut draws 40,000 possible versions of the rest of the count from those uncertainties and counts how often YES ends above 50%. That's the chance on the needle. The simulations are seeded from the data, so everyone sees the same number for the same update.

    The wobble

    On the Live count tab the wobble is decoration. Here it means something: the needle wobbles across the range of chances Peanut gets when it nudges its assumptions about the uncounted ballots up and down by a typical amount. A wide wobble means the chance itself is shaky; when the count is nearly done, the needle sits still.

    Does it work?

    We tested Peanut on thousands of made-up election nights. When the made-up world matches Peanut's assumptions, its 80% ranges contain the final result about 80% of the time. When the world is much more chaotic than Peanut assumes, Peanut gets overconfident, so the assumptions above are set on the wide side on purpose.

    What could go wrong

    • This election could break differently from past ones. Measure Y is a parcel tax paid by property owners, with an exemption for senior homeowners who apply, and that's unusual.
    • The county could count in a different order than usual, or post a correction.
    • If the first report we capture isn't the 8:10 PM early mail (say the scraper missed it), the baseline is muddier and Peanut widens its ranges.

    Want to argue with Peanut?

    Every assumption lives in one file, data/peanut-priors.json, in the project's GitHub repository. Suggest a change there with your reasoning (aggregate numbers and public sources only). The model itself is in js/peanut-model.js and the tests in tests/.

    How PEANUT works

    PEANUT (Peninsula Election Analysis & Needle, Unofficial Tally) is a just-for-fun forecast for people who like election-night needles. The Live count tab shows only the county's actual numbers; this tab estimates where those numbers will end up. Peanut's mood shows how sure the forecast is, not who's ahead.

    In short: Peanut takes the county's YES and NO totals, assumes the uncounted ballots will lean the way similar ballots have in past county elections, updates that as the count comes in, plays out the rest of the count 20,000 times and shows how often YES ends above 50%.

    What goes in

    Only what San Mateo County publishes for Measure Y at each update: the YES and NO totals and the county's "Website Updated" time.

    The idea: three groups of ballots

    • 8:10 PM: early mail. The first report is every mail ballot the county received through Sunday, Nov 1. Its YES share is Peanut's baseline.
    • Election night (9 PM, then hourly): in-person ballots. These updates add only ballots cast at vote centers. In past county elections those voters were less supportive of local measures than mail voters, so Peanut expects YES to slip during the night.
    • Nov 5 to Nov 16: late ballots. Mail dropped off on Election Day or arriving by Nov 10, plus provisional ballots and signature fixes. Late mail tends to come from younger voters, and on property taxes late ballots have run more YES than early mail, so Peanut expects YES to recover after election night.

    Each group gets a "lean": how many points more or less YES it is than the early mail. Peanut starts each lean from county history and updates it from every new county update (a standard Bayesian update; small updates count for less, and the lean is allowed to drift a little from one update to the next). The two leans are linked: if in-person ballots come in well below what Peanut expects, it also lowers what it expects from late ballots (and raises it if they come in with more YES), because in county history the measures whose in-person ballots fell furthest below the early mail also had late ballots with the least YES.

    What county history says

    • In-person ballots run less YES. Across 70 San Mateo County measures since 2020 with at least 200 in-person votes, in-person ballots ran about 4.5 points less YES than all mail ballots; on Belmont's own city measures the gap was 15.5 points in 2022 and 6.9 in 2024. Measured against the early mail alone, as Peanut does, the gap ran from about 1 to 5 points on the county's 2022 school bonds to about 16 to 17 on its 2022 hotel taxes, including Belmont's Measure K. (Computed from the county's certified results and election-night news counts.)
    • Late ballots have moved property taxes toward YES. In 12 county property-tax measures (parcel taxes, school bonds and one city bond) where an election-night count could be paired with the final count, YES gained every time, by 0.8 to 7.3 points. In the March 2020 primary, YES finished 2 to 10 points higher than the county's own 8:56 PM election-night snapshot on all seven school measures. On the county's Nov 2022 school bonds, late ballots ran about 3 to 6 points more YES than the early mail. Belmont's own city taxes that weren't property taxes barely moved after election night: against the early mail, late ballots on Belmont's 2022 hotel tax ran about 2 points less YES. (Pairs compiled from local news reports and the county's results.)
    • Measure Y is unusual. It's a citizen-initiated special tax on building square footage that passes with a simple majority, with an exemption for senior homeowners who apply. Peanut can't tell yet whether it will behave like the county's school taxes, where late ballots ran more YES, or like Belmont's 2022 hotel tax, whose late ballots ran about 2 points less YES than the early mail, so it assumes a late shift between the two, closer to the school taxes.

    How many votes are left

    Belmont has about 17,400 registered voters. Peanut combines a turnout estimate (about 11,300 votes on Measure Y, give or take 11%) with how much of the final count the county usually has done by each release. The 8:10 PM report and each post-election update pin this down a little more (the in-person updates on election night don't change it); by the last scheduled update only a few percent should remain.

    Then it simulates

    Peanut plays out 20,000 versions of the rest of the count from those uncertainties, with fat tails so big surprises stay possible, and counts how often YES ends above 50%. That's the chance on the needle. Every forecast uses the same random numbers, so everyone sees the same chance for the same count, and the number on the needle changes only when the count or an assumption changes. An assumption change redraws every past point on the chart.

    What the zones mean

    Tossup: a 40 to 60% chance. Leans YES: 61 to 75% (Leans NO: 25 to 39%). Likely YES: 76 to 95% (Likely NO: 5 to 24%). Very likely YES: 96% or more (Very likely NO: 4% or less). Even "Likely" is far from certain: at 80%, Measure Y would still fail about 1 time in 5.

    When Peanut calls it

    Peanut confirms that Measure Y passes only on county updates from Thu, Nov 5 on, and only when YES leads by more than Peanut's worst case for the votes still out: 4.5 standard deviations above its turnout estimate, the "at most" figure on this page. Then YES wins even if every one of those votes is a NO, so the call doesn't depend on how the last ballots lean, only on Peanut's estimate of how many are left. That is what "a mathematical certainty under Peanut's methodology" means, and the certainty is only as good as that estimate. In 3,200 made-up elections, including 1,000 that were 1.6 times as uncertain as Peanut assumes and 600 where the county counted 7 points slower than usual, Peanut made a call in 782 and never called one wrong. But when the county counted slower, more votes were really left than Peanut's "at most" in about one call in four (45 of 166), and in 6 of those every remaining vote being a NO would have sunk YES; those calls were right only because late ballots never break all one way. Peanut has no post-election report history from past measures, so the bound is untested on real late counts; on 32 early counts from 23 past San Mateo County measures, all but one from election night, it was at least 1.4 times the votes really left, and Peanut would not have called any of them. It usually calls a win of 60% YES or more by the Mon, Nov 16 update, about six in ten of 55 to 60% wins by then, and closer wins usually not before the county certifies. On certification day Peanut still allows for a little over 2% more votes than the count shows, so a small lead on a complete count is not called either. Only the county's certification is official. If the county counted slower than in recent elections, more votes could be left than Peanut thinks.

    The wobble

    On the Live count tab the wobble is just for fun. Here it means something. Peanut reruns the forecast 96 times with its assumptions (how the uncounted ballots lean, how fast the county counts, how many people vote) spread across their own uncertainty, and the needle wanders across the middle half of the chances that come out. The number on the needle already averages over all of these; the wobble shows how much it would move if Peanut's starting assumptions were off. A wide wobble means the chance itself depends a lot on assumptions; when the outcome no longer depends on them, the needle sits still. The button under the needle turns it off.

    What moves the needle

    • Until the Thu, Nov 5 update, the chance mostly reflects how much more YES the late ballots will be. In a close race, each point of that assumption is worth about 5 points of chance at 8:10 PM and up to about 8 by the end of election night.
    • On election night the in-person ballots matter too, through their link to late ballots.
    • By Mon, Nov 9 it mostly reflects how much late batches can differ from each other and, if YES trails, how many ballots are left.
    • Turnout and counting pace usually matter little while YES leads and late ballots are expected to run more YES: about a point, sometimes 2 to 4. When the side behind needs the ballots still to come to catch up, turnout and pace decide how many are left, so in a close race they can move the chance by 2 to 9 points, even on election night.

    Does it work?

    We replayed 23 real San Mateo County ballot questions from 2020 to 2026 (21 local measures, a city council recall and the state's March 2020 school bond, Proposition 13), giving Peanut only partial counts from election night (one from two days later), with its turnout guess deliberately 10% off. For eight Nov 2022 measures, the 8:10 PM mail-only count had to be rebuilt from news reports and the final in-person totals. Peanut's 80% ranges contained the certified result in 26 of 32 forecasts, and every forecast made after the first update. On property taxes and school bonds the final results still came in about 2.6 points more YES than Peanut's middle estimate, mostly in two primaries with unusually large late surges. These same elections helped set Peanut's assumptions, so expect it to do somewhat worse on a new one. On made-up election nights drawn from Peanut's own assumptions, its 80% ranges caught the result about 9 times in 10. That's cautious on purpose: Peanut also budgets for surprises those made-up nights don't contain. When the made-up nights were 60% more chaotic than Peanut assumes, its 80% ranges still caught the result nearly 4 times in 5.

    What could go wrong

    • The needle may fall sharply on election night. In-person voters in the 2022 midterm were much less supportive of local measures, and a big in-person slide also lowers what Peanut expects from late ballots, so the needle can drop overnight and recover from Thu, Nov 5 if late ballots still run more YES than the early mail.
    • Late ballots might not run more YES than the early mail this time. If they match it, Peanut will overstate YES's chances until the first post-election update on Thu, Nov 5 shows it.
    • The county could count in a different order than usual, or post a correction.
    • If the 8:10 PM report is missed or stamped late, the baseline includes some in-person ballots and is a bit muddier.

    Changes during the count

    From the first report until the county certifies, the assumptions change only to fix a real error, and every change to a number is listed here. Changes to the assumptions after the first report on Nov 3: none so far.
